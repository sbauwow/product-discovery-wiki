# Competitive Analysis and Positioning

> Sources: April Dunford (*Obviously Awesome*), Geoffrey Moore (*Crossing the Chasm*), Richard Rumelt (*Good Strategy / Bad Strategy*), Hamilton Helmer (*7 Powers*), pragmatic PM / founder practice. Discovery is not only about user pain. It is also about why someone should choose you over the alternatives they already have.

## Core thesis

Most teams do one of two bad versions of competitive analysis:
- they ignore competitors completely ("we have no competition"), or
- they build feature-comparison spreadsheets nobody uses.

Both miss the point.

Good competitive analysis answers:
1. What alternatives are customers actually using now?
2. On what dimensions do they compare us?
3. Where can we win credibly?
4. How should we position the product so the right customers immediately understand its value?

The goal is not encyclopedic market knowledge. The goal is sharper product and GTM choices.

## Start with alternatives, not competitors

Customers do not compare you only to direct competitors.

They compare you to:
- direct competitors
- adjacent tools
- internal workflows
- spreadsheets
- agencies / consultants
- doing nothing

This is the first mistake many teams make.

Example:
- A modern analytics product may think its competitors are Mixpanel and Amplitude.
- In reality, the buyer may be comparing against:
  - Amplitude
  - internal SQL + dashboards
  - an ops analyst
  - not solving the problem this quarter

Your real market position depends on the alternatives customers actually use.

## The alternative landscape

### 1. Direct competitors
Products serving the same buyer with a similar product shape.

### 2. Indirect competitors
Products solving the same underlying problem in a different way.

### 3. Substitutes
Non-product alternatives:
- manual labor
- consultants
- email
- meetings
- Excel
- inertia

### 4. Status quo
The strongest competitor in many categories.

If the pain is weak or the switching cost is high, the customer chooses nothing.

## Why this matters in discovery

Without alternative analysis, teams misread discovery data.

### 1. They over-index on feature requests
A user says, "Can you add X?"

But the real question is:
> Is X the reason we lose, or just how this person imagines catching up to what they already know?

### 2. They confuse interest with willingness to switch
People often like new ideas. They still stay with the incumbent.

### 3. They solve a real pain in a strategically bad way
You can satisfy a user need while positioning yourself as a worse copy of the market leader.

### 4. They build for comparison tables instead of product advantage
Competing line-by-line often erases differentiation.

## Positioning: what it really is

Positioning is not a slogan.

Positioning is the act of deliberately defining:
- what category or comparison set you belong in,
- who the product is for,
- what value it delivers,
- why it is better for that use case than the alternatives.

April Dunford's practical framing:

```
For [target customer]
who [has this problem / need]
our product is a [category]
that [key value / differentiated outcome].
Unlike [primary alternative],
it [key difference].
```

Example:

> For product-led SaaS teams with a messy activation funnel, our product is an activation-diagnosis platform that helps PMs identify why users fail to reach value. Unlike generic product analytics tools, it is designed around activation debugging workflows rather than open-ended dashboarding.

Good positioning changes what buyers compare you against.

## Category matters because comparison matters

Positioning is partly a category-choice problem.

If buyers put you in the wrong category, they judge you on the wrong dimensions.

Example:
- If you are positioned as "another analytics dashboard," buyers ask about chart flexibility, dashboard sharing, and SQL depth.
- If you are positioned as "activation diagnosis for PLG teams," buyers ask about time-to-insight, bottleneck detection, and onboarding interventions.

Same product. Different comparison frame.

This is why category and positioning are discovery issues, not just marketing issues.

## Feature comparisons vs strategic dimensions

Feature matrices are not useless. They are just shallow if used alone.

A stronger way is to compare on strategic dimensions customers actually care about:
- time-to-value
- implementation effort
- switching cost
- breadth vs depth
- workflow fit
- team maturity required
- trust / compliance
- total cost
- speed of insight
- human support / services

Example:

```
                     Incumbent A   Incumbent B   Us
Implementation time      slow          medium      fast
Customizability          high          medium      low
Activation workflow fit  low           medium      high
Analyst required         yes           sometimes   no
Time-to-first-insight    slow          medium      fast
```

This is much more useful than comparing 47 checkboxes.

## The positioning workflow

### Step 1. Identify actual alternatives from real customer behavior
Ask in interviews:
- What do you use today?
- What nearly made you choose something else?
- What would you do if our product disappeared tomorrow?
- What delayed solving this problem?

This produces a real alternative set.

### Step 2. Identify the buyer's comparison dimensions
Do not assume them.

Look for repeated buying criteria:
- speed
- safety
- flexibility
- simplicity
- price
- compatibility
- trust
- ease of rollout

### Step 3. Decide where you can win
Not where you can tie. Not where you can someday tie. Where you can win now.

This often means narrowing the use case or segment.

### Step 4. Choose your comparison set deliberately
Sometimes the best move is:
- compete directly in a known category,
- define a tighter subcategory,
- or reframe the problem entirely.

### Step 5. Write the positioning statement
Short enough to use. Specific enough to exclude.

### Step 6. Test it on real prospects
A good positioning statement should:
- attract the right people,
- repel the wrong ones,
- shorten explanation time,
- improve conversion quality.

## Good competitive analysis questions

### For direct users / buyers
- What do you use today?
- What do you like about it?
- What frustrates you?
- Why haven't you switched already?
- What would make switching feel worth it?

### For lost deals
- What did they choose instead?
- What dimension did they optimize for?
- Were we missing capability, credibility, ecosystem, or urgency?

### For internal strategy
- Where are incumbents structurally strong?
- Where are they bloated or slow?
- What customer segment do they serve poorly?
- What constraints stop them from copying us quickly?

That last question connects directly to [7 Powers](seven-powers.md) and [product strategy](product-strategy.md).

## Switching costs matter more than many teams think

A product can be better and still lose.

Why?
- migration pain
- training cost
- data lock-in
- workflow disruption
- political cost inside the customer org
- risk of being blamed if the new tool fails

Competitive analysis that ignores switching cost is fantasy.

A common winning move is not "be better overall."
It is:
> be meaningfully better on one painful dimension while reducing the switching burden enough that change becomes rational.

## Common positioning patterns

### 1. Better product in same category
Use when buyers already understand the category and your improvement is obvious.

### 2. More focused product for one segment
Use when general-purpose incumbents exist but underserve a niche.

### 3. Different workflow / operating model
Use when your advantage is speed, simplicity, automation, or a new interaction model.

### 4. New category / subcategory
Use when direct comparison hurts you and the product really is judged better by a different frame.

This is where [Blue Ocean](blue-ocean.md) and [Crossing the Chasm](crossing-the-chasm.md) start to matter.

## Common failure modes

### "We have no competitors"
False almost always. The competitor may be a spreadsheet, budget freeze, or internal team.

### Competitor obsession
You become reactive and copy-driven instead of strategy-driven.

### Feature table trap
The team builds parity instead of advantage.

### Positioning for everyone
A message broad enough to attract everyone usually persuades no one.

### Ignoring the status quo
Many losses are to inaction, not to another vendor.

### Wrong category choice
You let buyers compare you on dimensions where incumbents are already strongest.

### Confusing what customers say with what they buy on
People say many things. Real buying criteria show up in closed-won / closed-lost behavior.

## Good artifacts to create

### 1. Alternatives map
One page:
- direct competitors
- indirect competitors
- substitutes
- status quo

### 2. Comparison-dimensions sheet
List the 5–10 dimensions buyers actually use.

### 3. Win/Loss memo
Monthly or quarterly summary of:
- why we won
- why we lost
- what alternatives beat us
- where the pattern is changing

### 4. Positioning brief
Contains:
- target customer
- category
- differentiated value
- alternatives
- proof points
- what we are explicitly not

## Heuristic

If your product can only be described as:
> "like [incumbent], but with more features"

then your positioning is weak and your strategy is probably weak too.
