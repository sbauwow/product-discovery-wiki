# Disruption Theory

> Source: Clayton Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997), refined in *The Innovator's Solution* (2003) and later papers. The most-cited and most-misused theory in business strategy. Explains why dominant incumbents lose to seemingly inferior new entrants.

## The thesis

> Incumbent companies are killed by **innovations that initially appear inferior** but improve faster than expected, eventually displacing the incumbent's better-on-paper product.

The paradox: the incumbent's *strengths* become its weakness. Listening to its best customers, optimizing margins, and improving its core product — all "best practice" — actively prevents it from responding to disruption.

## Two innovation types

### Sustaining innovation
Improves an existing product along dimensions current customers value.
- Faster CPUs in PCs
- Better cameras in iPhones
- Larger screens in TVs

Incumbents *win* sustaining innovation. They have the customer relationships, the resources, the manufacturing scale.

### Disruptive innovation
Introduces a *different* value proposition — usually worse on the metrics current customers value, but better on different metrics that some non-customers (or low-end customers) prefer.

- PCs vs minicomputers (PCs were "worse" but cheaper, easier)
- Digital cameras vs film (lower image quality initially, but instant + free)
- Netflix DVDs vs Blockbuster (slower delivery, but no late fees + bigger catalog)
- Salesforce vs Siebel (less customizable, but cheaper + web-based)
- Tesla vs ICE (worse range/charging at first, but instant torque + zero-emission)

Disruptive products start in **footholds** the incumbent doesn't care about (low-end customers, or non-consumers). They improve over time. Eventually they're "good enough" for the mainstream. Incumbent loses.

## Two disruption flavors

### Low-end disruption
Targets customers the incumbent overserves at the low end.

```
Performance
   │  ──────  high-end customer needs
   │ ╱
   │╱        ── incumbent product (overshooting)
   │
   │       ── low-end customer needs
   │     ╱
   │   ╱   ── disruptor product (undershoots high-end, hits low-end)
   │ ╱
   └──────────────→ time
```

Incumbent's product gets *better* faster than customers need. Eventually serves only the high end profitably. Disruptor enters at the low end, captures the overserved customers, then improves toward the high end.

Example: Steel mini-mills vs integrated steel mills. Mini-mills started with rebar (lowest-margin); incumbents happily ceded it. Mini-mills improved; took I-beams; then sheet steel. Eventually displaced integrated mills entirely.

### New-market disruption
Targets *non-consumers* — people who couldn't afford or didn't have access to the incumbent's product.

```
Sony Walkman: tiny radios for people who couldn't carry stereos
PCs: computers for people who couldn't afford minicomputers
Mobile phones: phones for people without office phones
Netflix early: watching at home for people not going to video stores
```

Disruptor doesn't compete with incumbent in the existing market. Creates a new, growing market. Eventually the new market subsumes the old.

## Why incumbents can't respond

Christensen's "innovator's dilemma": incumbents *do* see disruption coming. They *can't* respond because:

### 1. Customer focus
Their best customers explicitly don't want the disruptive product (it's worse on their metrics). Listening to customers — usually a virtue — points away from the disruption.

### 2. Margin protection
The disruptor's market is small and low-margin. Investing there cannibalizes the incumbent's high-margin business. Rational financial analysis kills the disruptive bet.

### 3. Resource allocation
Internal teams compete for budget. The 200-person product line generating $1B is "more important" than the 5-person experimental team. The 5-person team starves.

### 4. Capability gap
Incumbent's processes optimized for sustaining innovation. Disruption requires different cost structures, different sales motions, different cultures. Hard to spawn from within.

### 5. Public-market pressure
Quarterly earnings demand growth in core. Disruption requires patience and small-scale starts. CEOs who try to disrupt themselves lose to activist investors.

## The "Innovator's Solution"

Christensen's prescription (2003, less cited than the diagnosis):

1. **Spawn a separate org** with its own P&L and culture
2. **Match the disruption's economics** (low-cost, low-overhead)
3. **Don't sell to existing customers** — find non-consumers or low-end users
4. **Allow the new org to grow autonomously** — don't impose corporate processes
5. **Plan for cannibalization** — the new business will eventually eat the old one; that's the goal
6. **Commit before the threat is obvious** — by the time disruption is visible, it's too late

Most incumbents fail to do these. The few that succeed (IBM into services, Netflix into streaming, Amazon into AWS) are exceptions, often founder-led at decision time.

## Misuse of the term

"Disruption" is the most-abused word in tech. Most "disruptive" products are not, in Christensen's sense:

| Often called "disruption" | Actually |
|---|---|
| Uber vs taxis | Mostly **sustaining** for high-end customers; some new-market for low-end |
| iPhone vs Nokia | Sustaining innovation that displaced a sustaining incumbent — not classical disruption |
| Tesla vs Ford | Currently sustaining (high-end first); will be disruptive only if cheap models commoditize |
| ChatGPT vs Google Search | Possibly disruption, possibly sustaining; theory itself is contested for AI |

Christensen himself was rigorous about distinguishing "disruption" from "really good competition." Most uses of the word are sloppy.

The test: is the disruptor entering at the *low end* or with *non-consumers*, with a product *worse on incumbent metrics* but better on others? If yes, disruption. If it's just "better product wins", not disruption — sustaining innovation by a new entrant.

## Disruption + Crossing the Chasm

These frameworks don't compete; they describe different layers:

| | [Crossing the Chasm](crossing-the-chasm.md) | Disruption |
|---|---|---|
| Lens | Adoption sequence within a market | Competitive dynamics across markets |
| Question | How to get from early adopters to mainstream? | Why does the new entrant beat the incumbent? |
| Time | 2-5 years | 5-15 years |
| Useful for | Startup go-to-market | Strategic choice of attack vector |

A disruption-attempt product still has to cross its own chasm within the disrupted market.

## Connection to product discovery

Disruption theory shapes [strategy](product-strategy.md):

- **For challengers**: identifying overshoot in incumbent products → opportunity for disruption
- **For incumbents**: recognizing low-end / new-market threats early → defensive bets
- **For VCs**: pattern matching on disruptive vs sustaining bets

[JTBD](jobs-to-be-done.md) integrates with disruption: disruptors often serve the same *job* with a different solution that's worse on some dimensions, better on others. The job is stable; the solution shifts.

## Common failure modes

- **Calling all change "disruption"** — vocabulary inflation; theory loses meaning
- **Defending against disruption with sustaining innovation** — making the existing product better when the threat is structurally different
- **Imitation theater** — incumbent launches a "lab" or "innovation team" without isolating it from corporate processes; lab gets crushed
- **Ignoring low-end / nonconsumers** — those segments are where disruption starts; ignoring them is incumbent default behavior
- **Late, panicked moves** — by the time disruption is mainstream news, the window for incumbent response has usually closed

## When disruption framing matters

✅ Strategic decisions about attack vector or defense
✅ Long-horizon investment
✅ Markets with clear performance overshoot (incumbent product more capable than mainstream needs)
✅ Adjacent technologies with different cost structures

## When less useful

❌ Tactical product decisions (use [JTBD](jobs-to-be-done.md), [discovery](continuous-discovery.md))
❌ Markets without entrenched incumbents
❌ Pure innovation (where there's nothing to disrupt)
