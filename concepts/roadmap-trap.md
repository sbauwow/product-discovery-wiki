# The Roadmap Trap

> The most common artifact-level dysfunction in product orgs. The roadmap, when treated as a *commitment* rather than a *bet*, locks teams into output thinking.

## The pattern

```
Quarterly planning
    ↓
Feature list with dates committed
    ↓
Engineering signs up to deliver
    ↓
Sales sells features ahead of ship
    ↓
Discovery shows feature is wrong
    ↓
... ship anyway, because committed
```

Once the roadmap is a contract, [discovery](continuous-discovery.md) becomes theater. Even strong evidence can't redirect a team that has political/sales obligations to ship X by Y.

## How roadmaps degrade

A *good* roadmap (rare):
- Outcomes per quarter, not features
- Ranges of possibility, not dates
- Updated based on learning
- Internally framed as "current best bets"

A *typical* roadmap (common):
- Features with dates
- Sold to customers as commitments
- Treated as fixed until next planning cycle
- Updated through political fight, not evidence

The degradation happens because:
- **Sales** needs concrete things to sell → demands feature names + dates
- **Execs** want predictability → demand commitments
- **Customers** want timelines → demand specifics
- **Boards** want progress → see features as proof of progress

Each pressure is reasonable. Combined, they convert "directional bet" into "binding contract."

## Symptoms of the trap

- The roadmap document is a Gantt chart
- Quarterly reviews ask "did we ship what we committed?" not "did we move outcomes?"
- Teams negotiate scope/dates, never strategic relevance
- Discovery happens after the roadmap is set
- Stakeholders react to roadmap changes as broken promises
- Sales pre-sells unbuilt features, locking the team in

## Why "agile" doesn't fix it

Many orgs do agile delivery (sprints, standups) on top of waterfall planning (locked annual roadmap). The result: agile *delivery* of *predetermined output*. The core problem — locking outcomes before discovery — remains.

This is what Marty Cagan calls "agile process, waterfall thinking."

## The Cagan/Torres alternatives

### Now / Next / Later

```
NOW (committed)        NEXT (likely)         LATER (ideas)
- 2-3 outcomes         - 3-5 outcomes         - vague areas
  with high confidence   under exploration     for future quarters
```

Time horizons of decreasing certainty. Stakeholders see directionality without false precision.

### Outcome-based roadmap

```
Q1: Increase activation 30→45%        ← outcome
Q2: Reduce churn 8→5%                 ← outcome
Q3: Add 1 new revenue stream          ← outcome
```

Features are NOT named. The team picks them based on discovery.

### Bet-shaped roadmap (Basecamp's *Shape Up*)

Each "bet" is a problem statement + appetite (time available) + rough solution. Bets are committed for one cycle (6 weeks), then re-bet. No multi-quarter feature backlog.

## How to escape (from inside)

If you inherit a feature roadmap:

1. **Add outcome columns** to existing roadmap docs ("what does shipping this *do*?")
2. **Track discovery alongside delivery** — show where evidence has changed direction
3. **Reframe one quarter as outcomes** — pilot with one team, show results
4. **Educate sales/CS** — "we'll commit to outcomes; features are subject to discovery"
5. **Build trust on small bets** — leadership won't trust outcome roadmaps until they see them work

## Roadmaps still serve a purpose

This isn't an argument against roadmaps. Orgs need:
- Communicated direction (employees, investors, customers)
- Cross-team coordination
- Strategic narrative

The fix is roadmap *content*, not roadmap *existence*. Outcome-shaped, time-windowed, bet-framed roadmaps work. Feature-shaped, date-committed roadmaps don't.
