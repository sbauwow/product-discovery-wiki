# Three Horizons of Growth

> Source: McKinsey & Company, *The Alchemy of Growth* (1999). A portfolio framework for innovation: how companies should simultaneously invest in **defending today**, **building tomorrow**, and **exploring next**.

## The three horizons

```
Profit / Revenue
   │
   │              H3 ── exploratory bets, small/zero revenue
   │             ╱            (5-10% of investment)
   │           ╱
   │         ╱              H2 ── emerging business
   │       ╱                ╱       (15-25% of investment)
   │     ╱               ╱
   │  H1 ─────────────────────────  core business
   │       ╱                          (60-80% of investment)
   │   ╱
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────→ time
        now           1-3 yrs          3-5+ yrs
```

### Horizon 1 — Defend & Extend the Core
- Today's revenue-generating business
- Optimize, defend, incrementally improve
- 60-80% of investment
- Short-cycle outcomes (quarterly)
- KPIs: revenue, margin, retention, NPS

### Horizon 2 — Build Emerging Businesses
- New growth lines visible but not yet profitable
- Adjacent markets, new segments, new product lines
- 15-25% of investment
- Medium-cycle outcomes (1-3 years)
- KPIs: customer acquisition, revenue ramp, validated business model

### Horizon 3 — Create Viable Options
- Speculative bets on the long-term future
- R&D, experiments, small explorations, partnerships
- 5-10% of investment
- Long-cycle outcomes (3-5+ years)
- KPIs: validated learning, optionality, strategic positioning

## The thesis

Sustained growth requires investing in **all three horizons simultaneously.** Companies that focus only on H1 (the core) appear healthy short-term but get disrupted long-term. Companies that focus only on H3 (moonshots) burn cash without revenue. Companies that ignore H2 starve themselves of growth.

Rough investment heuristic (varies by stage):
- Mature company: 70-20-10
- Growth-stage: 60-30-10
- Early stage: 30-50-20 (most investment in still-emerging core)

Pre-PMF startups don't fit this model — they only have H1 (the bet itself).

## The disruption trap

Ties directly to [disruption theory](disruption-theory.md):

- H1 (core) is where the company makes money. Optimize it.
- H2 (emerging) is where competitors disrupt you. Often unprofitable in early years; rational accounting kills it.
- H3 (exploration) is where the *next* H2 comes from.

The trap: every quarter, leadership chooses to defend H1 because it's profitable today. H2 budget gets cut. H3 is "frivolous." Three years later, the disruption arrives — and the company has no H2 to compete with it.

Christensen's [innovator's dilemma](disruption-theory.md) is the disease; three-horizons portfolio is the cure.

## Per-horizon discipline

Each horizon needs different *practices* and *measures*:

| | H1 | H2 | H3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision-making | Optimization | Validation | Exploration |
| Cycle | Quarterly | Annual | Multi-year |
| Investment style | Operational | Venture-style | R&D / options |
| Failure tolerance | Low | Medium | High |
| Right metrics | Revenue, margin | LTV, CAC, retention curves | Validated learning |
| Discovery practice | [A/B](ab-testing.md), [funnel](funnel-analysis.md) | [PMF](pmf.md), [continuous discovery](continuous-discovery.md) | [Lean Startup](lean-startup.md), [RAT](rat.md) |
| Leadership style | Performance management | Bet management | Option management |

Applying H1 metrics to H3 work kills H3. Applying H3 metrics to H1 fails to maintain the core. Different horizons need different rules.

## Worked example: an e-commerce incumbent

```
H1 (60% investment): Core marketplace
  - SEO optimization
  - Conversion rate improvements
  - Logistics efficiency
  - Customer acquisition channels

H2 (25% investment): Adjacent businesses
  - B2B wholesale platform (1 year in, ~5% of revenue, growing)
  - International expansion (Brazil, India)
  - Subscription / membership tier

H3 (15% investment): Exploratory bets
  - Voice commerce experiments
  - Generative-AI shopping assistant prototype
  - Drone delivery pilot in 2 cities
```

A balanced portfolio. Five years out, one of the H3 bets may become H2, or one of the H2 bets may become a new core.

## Connection to product strategy

[Product strategy](product-strategy.md) operates *within* a horizon. Different strategy at each horizon:

- H1 strategy = how to defend / extend the core
- H2 strategy = how to validate and scale the emerging business
- H3 strategy = how to learn cheaply about the future

Most product orgs run a strategy *only* for H1 — and then wonder why no H2 ever emerges.

## When three-horizons framing matters

✅ Mature companies with established core
✅ Annual / multi-year strategic planning
✅ Resource allocation across multiple lines of business
✅ Defending against disruption
✅ Boards / investor communication

## When less useful

❌ Pre-PMF startups (only H1 exists)
❌ Single-product focused companies that haven't found PMF
❌ Tactical, near-term decisions
❌ When "exploration" is being used as cover for unfocused spending

## Common failure modes

- **All H1, no H2 or H3** — defending the core perfectly while a competitor builds your replacement; classic disruption setup
- **All H3, no H1** — burn cash on speculation; no core to fund the bets
- **H2 starved** — leadership pays lip service to H2 but cuts its budget every cycle in favor of H1 returns
- **Bad measurement** — applying H1 metrics to H2 (e.g. demanding immediate ROI from emerging business)
- **No org separation** — H2/H3 work attempted within H1 teams; H1 urgency crushes H2/H3 every time
- **Misclassifying bets** — calling a small H1 improvement an "H3 innovation"; vocabulary inflation
- **Chasing all three at once with same budget** — three horizons aren't free; investment proportions matter

## Org structure implications

Many companies use [Team Topologies](team-topologies.md) by horizon:
- Stream-aligned teams own H1 work (and emerging H2 with care)
- Enabling teams spread practices across H2 work
- Complicated-subsystem teams may sit in H3 (e.g. ML research)
- Platform teams support across all horizons

Some companies separate H3 entirely (Google X, Amazon's Lab126, Meta's Reality Labs) to avoid H1 culture crushing H3 work.
